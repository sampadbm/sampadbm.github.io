*Three comments I came across, in reply to a student asking how to meet a partner. The first two give opposite advice; the third is about making time for a social life at all. These are other people's experiences, not mine.*

## First comment: look for shared curiosity

> Im going to say the opposite of a lot of people here. Dont go to random events. You will find someone who doesnt share your interests. Instead go to hack a thons or nerd conferences (I mean nerd in an endearing way as a fellow nerd). Being able to talk about the details of what you do is immensely gratifying in a relationship and prevents feelings of loneliness later when your spouse doesnt care.
>
> Also don't stress. I remember my early twenties and it was great. Finally people who share my interests. Then i was devastated in my midtwenties when all my friends graduated and got married but I was still single. Graduated and entered the workforce and stayed single into my late twenties. There are genuinely cool people you will still meet in life. Dont stress. Dont let the pressure of seeing friends get married and start families trouble you.
>
> I ended up meeting the woman who would become my wife when i was in my late twenties. It turns out we were both curious and that was the most important thing. We literally do math together on the weekends for fun.

## Second comment: meet people outside your world

> I repeat one of the suggestions:
>
> Go meet people outside of your university and workplace. This is no doubt a daunting aspect, but, it is rewarding. If you are weird, don't try to hide it. You will at the least make new friends, who like you for who you are. They may not be anything like you, but that turned out to be a good thing for me.
>
> I have dated many people, and absolutely most of them had nothing to do with STEM. I have dated only one ~mathematician (I'm not opposed to it. But it hasn't happened is all), she is a computer scientist.

## The idea

**From the first comment:**

- **Choose where you look.** Instead of random events, go where people share your interests, such as hackathons and technical conferences. Being able to talk about the details of your work with a partner is deeply satisfying, and it protects against a particular loneliness later: having a spouse who doesn't care about what you do.
- **Don't panic about timing.** Watching friends graduate, marry, and start families while you are still single is hard, but there are still genuinely good people to meet later. The author met their wife in their late twenties.
- **Curiosity is the thing that matters.** What made it work was that both of them were curious. They do math together on weekends for fun.

**From the second comment:**

- **Go beyond your university and workplace.** Meeting people outside your usual circles is daunting but rewarding.
- **Don't hide being weird.** At the very least you will make friends who like you for who you are.
- **Difference can be good.** The people you meet may be nothing like you, and for this author that turned out well. Most of the people they dated had nothing to do with STEM.

**From the third comment:**

- **Say yes to invitations.** Beach trips, movies, bowling, hanging out: make time for them and for meeting new people. Missed chances turn into lasting regret.
- **Work is not enough on its own.** A degree or honor on the wall won't comfort you after a bad dream, bring you tea when you're sick, or hold you when you need to be loved.
- **Work hard, play hard.** Working hard toward your goals is fine, as long as you also make room to enjoy life.

**Putting them together.** The two comments disagree about where to look: one says go where people share your interests, the other says go outside your usual world and stay open to people unlike you. Both still point toward the same end: someone who values you for who you are and what you care about. Perhaps the reconciliation is that shared *curiosity* matters more than shared *field*. Someone outside STEM can still be curious in a way that matches yours. The third comment comes before either strategy: neither works if studying leaves no time to show up anywhere.

Both also relate to the [propinquity effect](#2026-11-01) entry, which says bonds come from repeated proximity, whoever happens to be there. The first comment adds: choose the room so that the people in it already share what you care about. The two combine well: become a regular somewhere full of people with your interests.
