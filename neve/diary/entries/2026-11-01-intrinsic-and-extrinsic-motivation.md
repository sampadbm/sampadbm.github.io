People often look for problems to solve as a means to an end: a stronger resume, standing out, getting noticed. There is nothing inherently wrong with that, as long as the order is right. The intrinsic motivation, the fun of actually solving the problem, should come first. Extrinsic rewards like recognition and a standout record should be a side product of it, not the goal.

*What follows is my own view, drawn from personal experience and from working with people at both ends of this spectrum. It is not backed by systematic evidence.*

## Where extrinsic motivation stops working

Extrinsic motivation can carry you some distance in the short term. Even there, I think it helps less than it seems to, because work done mainly to be displayed is usually easy to recognize as such.

The bigger cost shows up over time. Solving any real problem takes a long time. If you treat problem solving as a means to an end, you will tend to filter out the harder and more interesting problems in favour of ones that are tractable, legible, and quick to show off. You end up optimizing for what looks good rather than for what matters.

## The counter-intuitive part

My feeling, and it is a feeling rather than an established fact, is that the less you chase extrinsic rewards, the more you end up rewarded in the long run. In the short run, following genuine interest can bring what look like large setbacks. Over a longer horizon, I suspect many of those setbacks turn out to be blessings in disguise.

Insights like this are hard to have early on, and I did not have them at first either. In my experience, though, the people who are the greatest pleasure to work with are the ones who care about the problem first and the resume second.
