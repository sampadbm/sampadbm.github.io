# Teaching Statement

I came to mathematics by a less direct route than I expected. I first wanted to study physics, then trained in electrical engineering and computer science, fields in which mathematics remained the language underlying the problems I cared about. Now, while finishing a PhD in Computer Science at USC, I am also working through an MS in Applied Mathematics. Across teaching assistantships in computer science and a mathematics department's data-science course, and several years mentoring high school and undergraduate students, I have learned that students understand mathematics best when they can see its connections, discover those connections for themselves, and test them through applications.

## Philosophy

Doing mathematics involves critical thinking, problem solving, recognizing patterns, and seeing connections between seemingly disparate phenomena — through abstraction and generalization, specialization and concrete examples, or both. Undergraduate training often emphasizes the first two while leaving the connective structure implicit. My teaching makes that structure visible, then invites students to discover it through carefully scaffolded individual and group work. Arriving at an insight for oneself, or with peers, can be deeply rewarding. It can also support autonomy, competence, and relatedness, the three basic psychological needs that foster intrinsic motivation (SDT, Ryan & Deci, 2000).

MATH 499, USC's inaugural undergraduate data-science course for mathematics majors, gave me an early chance to put this principle into practice. As a volunteer TA and guest lecturer, I taught linear regression, projections, and the SVD and mentored student projects. I could not assume the computing background common in CS and EE, so I built computational ideas from mathematical objects the students already knew. Once those connections became visible, students adapted quickly; their excitement at using familiar mathematics to answer concrete questions remains one of my most rewarding teaching experiences.

I would carry that approach into course design. A high-level concept map at the beginning of a course would connect its topics to one another and to prerequisites; smaller maps for each unit would let students locate new definitions within that larger structure. Rather than keeping linear algebra, probability, and calculus in separate boxes, I would cross those boundaries deliberately. For example, students in an introductory linear algebra, probability, or statistics course could plot the eigenvalue and eigenvector distributions of random matrices, moving from connected concepts to a question they can investigate themselves.

In class, I use group problem solving and guided exploration in which students anticipate a theorem or property before it is formally introduced. I also like to reverse roles: students give a short tutorial on a topic that excites them or design practice problems for their classmates. Explaining and designing problems gives them access to a mode of learning they rarely experience as students.

Applications complete the progression from connections to discovery. Dual numbers can make forward-mode automatic differentiation tangible in calculus; backpropagation can illuminate the multivariate chain rule; Monte Carlo experiments can connect probability and computation. To make such investigation practical at classroom scale, I built **LEAP** (Live Experiments for Active Pedagogy), an open-source framework through which a whole class can collaboratively run an experiment during a lecture and see everyone's results visualized in real time. A poster on LEAP was accepted at ACM SIGCSE 2026, and I plan to develop and pilot LEAP labs for core applied-mathematics courses.

AI makes this emphasis on reasoning and verification more important. Rather than treating AI use only as a discipline problem, I would ask students to explain or defend solutions, locate errors in AI-generated proofs, and do more live investigation. Assessment should place less weight on routine computation and more on problem formulation, modeling, interpretation, and verification.

## Experiences from TA, Tutoring and Mentoring

My teaching experience at university level so far has been earned almost entirely as a teaching assistant in computer science and machine learning. Six semesters across three different instructors in the same machine learning course (CS 567) meant adjusting to each instructor's emphasis and style. As Head TA for CS 467 (Foundations of Machine Learning) and CS 570 (Analysis of Algorithms), I was solely or primarily responsible for course operations: designing homework sets and practice exams from scratch, running weekly discussion sections, and handling grading and logistics for the course.

Outside formal courses, most of my teaching has been one-on-one mentoring. I guided high school and early undergraduate students through nonlinear dimensionality reduction to a research poster and through reinforcement-learning research. I have also mentored robotics and aerospace design projects and judged student presentations at the California Academy of Math and Science. My mentoring has taken different forms. I advised one student on possible directions for his undergraduate studies; he later added applied mathematics as a second major alongside computer science at MIT. I worked more closely with another, Sumedh, who became a co-author of LEAP and later received full undergraduate funding to pursue a major of his choice at Vanderbilt. These experiences shaped my practice of mapping connections before moving toward applications, while adapting the scaffolding to each student's background.

My earliest teaching experience was tutoring. From 2013, as an undergraduate, through 2018, while working as a researcher, I tutored high school students, and undergraduates in signals and systems and electrical engineering. I also volunteered as an instructor at a public school in India for an ATAL Tinkering Lab, which trains school students in hands-on electronics, robotics, and programming.
