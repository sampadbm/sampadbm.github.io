# Teaching Statement

Students understand mathematics best when they see its connections, discover them for themselves, and test them through applications. My path from physics through electrical engineering and computer science to applied mathematics taught me that mathematics is a common language across fields. I help students see that connective structure, investigate it, and put it to use.

## Philosophy

### Connections, exploration, and active learning

I organize my teaching around **connections**, **discovery**, and **application**. I relate a new idea to concepts students already know, use individual or group work to let them explore it before presenting it formally, and then ask them to test it through computation, modeling, or explanation. This process gives students room to develop the autonomy, competence, and relatedness that support intrinsic motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2000). I used this approach to help students who struggled with the algebraic view of the singular value decomposition (SVD) in MATH 499, where I served as a volunteer TA and guest lecturer. Presenting the SVD as projection, stretching, and change of basis helped some students connect the algebra to the geometry, while others found the ideas easier to understand by exploring them in Python. This experience showed me the value of offering algebraic, geometric, and computational viewpoints for understanding the same idea. Similarly, theory and application offer complementary perspectives. Through real-world examples and mathematical modeling, I show how a concrete problem can be translated into the mathematical structures we are studying.

I use concept maps to organize a course. Before introducing a topic, I show students where it fits and which earlier ideas it draws on; afterward, we return to the map to connect what they have learned to the rest of the course. In foundational courses such as linear algebra, calculus, probability, and statistics, I hope to include investigations that connect these subjects. Students might, for example, generate random matrices, plot their eigenvalue and eigenvector distributions, and use linear algebra and probability to explain the patterns they find. They may also work toward a theorem in groups, give a short tutorial, or write practice problems for one another. I built **LEAP** (Live Experiments for Active Pedagogy), an open-source framework that pools students' individual trials and displays the results in real time, to support this kind of active learning. Although LEAP has not yet been piloted in a classroom, a poster on the framework was accepted at ACM SIGCSE 2026, and I plan to develop it for applied mathematics classrooms.

### Assessment and access

I use explanations, practice problems, project work, and live questions to see how students reason, not only whether they reach the correct answer. Short, low-stakes checks help me decide whether to revisit a prerequisite, try another representation, or move forward. In graded work, I place weight on problem formulation, modeling choices, interpretation, and verification. I also structure group work around intermediate questions and present ideas algebraically, geometrically, verbally, and computationally so that students with different backgrounds have ways into the same problem.

### Teaching in the age of generative AI

As generative AI makes answers easier to obtain, reasoning and verification become more important. I plan to ask students to defend solutions, identify hidden assumptions, and find errors in AI-generated arguments. In this way, AI-generated work becomes material for mathematical critique rather than a substitute for it.

## Experiences from TA, Tutoring and Mentoring

My university teaching experience spans machine learning, algorithms, artificial intelligence, distributed systems, and mathematical data science. Teaching CS 567 across six semesters and three instructors required me to adapt to different curricular emphases while developing a stable sense of what students find difficult. As Head TA for CS 467 (Foundations of Machine Learning) and CS 570 (Analysis of Algorithms), I was responsible for designing homework sets and practice exams, leading weekly discussion sections, coordinating grading, and managing course operations. These roles taught me to connect the work of an individual lesson with the design and consistency of an entire course.

Mentoring has shown me how the same philosophy must change with the learner. I have guided high school and early undergraduate students through nonlinear dimensionality reduction, reinforcement-learning research, and robotics and aerospace design projects. At the outset, I map a project into conceptual dependencies and identify which pieces a student can already approach independently. As the student gains command, I shift from supplying structure to asking questions that require them to choose a direction, justify a method, or diagnose a failed attempt. One student I mentored later became a co-author of LEAP. With another, I discussed how computing and mathematics might fit together as the student planned an undergraduate program spanning both fields. In both cases, I was glad to see the students become more comfortable choosing a direction and explaining their decisions.
