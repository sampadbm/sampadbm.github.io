# Teaching Statement


### AI, Mathematics and Pedagogy
Mathematics as we know today is evolving rapidly as it makes contact with modern AI. In early 2020s, AI made rapid progress in natural language and since 2024 they have made rapid progress in tackling highschool math, then olympiad math and now making progress in frontier mathematics problems of the century. On the otherhand, mathematics and physics/applied mathematics have immense contributions in understanding how AI models achieve such feat under the umbrella of interpretability research in AI. Althought, computing and mathematics have always been close philosophically (since foundations were laid by Turing, Church, Shannon, Weiner, Neunmann, John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky and many other mathematicians) and numerical and scientific computing permeating every serious field of science and enginnering, this new kind of interaction between the fields is unprecedented. Unequivocally, AI is a paradigm shift in how every field - programming, medical science, robotics, and now mathematics and education. Pedagogy in mathematics has to keep pace with these developments and I view these changes positively. While concerns about students using AI to complete their assignments and exams are legitimate, I see it as a need for adaptation of learning outcomes and evaluation methodologies in light of the new  which our students will operate in.   




### Philosophy
Doing mathematics involves critical thinking, problem solving, recognizing patterns and realizing connections between seemingly disparate phenomena or problems by abstraction/generalization or by specialization/concretion or both. Critical thinking and problem solving are indispensible skills for doing mathematics and are usually a big part of the focus of an undergraduate training in mathematics, rightly so. However, in my experiences, often the third part was either neglected or delegated to an advanced course meanwhile the advanced course often already assumes familiarity with these interconnections. My approach to teaching emphasizes these interconnections and gives students a taste for them through **guided self/group-discovery**. The experience of arriving at an insight for oneself or in collaboration with peers can be deeply rewarding in intellectual pursuits and, when appropriately scaffolded, can support students’ sense of **autonomy, competence, and relatedness**—the three basic psychological needs associated with intrinsic and self-determined motivation in **Self-Determination Theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000)**. Every course begins with some set of concepts/objects described by construction or introduced via their properties, and then proceeding to examine these properties in more detail and finding the interconnections between the objects/properties. New properties/objects that might emerge from these discussions become the primary subject of the course. At the begining of every course, I like to provide in advance a high level concept map (could be a Venn diagrams, directed acyclic graphs, tables, etc.) containing the topics, their properties and interconnections to be discussed in that course which **explicitly maps the cncepts in the prerequisite course** to the that of the present course. Each chapter/week, a more detailed zoomed in concept map provides the interconnections of the various topics to be discussed that chapter/week. This helps students to brush up on the definitions and properties of the objects/concepts involved in advance. While usually courses like linear algebra, probability and calculus are taught in isolation, I prefer to introduce and highlight the interactions betweem them even in an introductory class for I believe these boundaries must be transgressed for a holistic understanding. As an example, introducing random matrices and their properties in an introductory linear algebra/probability/statistics course and have hands on computational exercise for plotting distributions of eigenvalues/eigenvectors of random matrices would lead to better engagement. 

I believe applications are indispensible part of the teaching and learning process. In my experience, some students are motivated to learn the concepts when presented in light of an application. I could introduce the concept of dual numbers and how they are used in forward mode automatic differentiation present in many neural network libraries in a calculus course. Similary, in a calculus-III/multivariate calculus class, introducing backpropagation algorithm as an application of the multivariate chain rule used in reverse mode automatic differentiation. In an statistics course, touch upon computational aspects of montecarlo sampling and basic information geometry widely used in guided random search and reinforcement learning algorithms. Or introducing some perturbation theory of matrices and show them connections to  neural network weight initializations and interpretability research. 

I prefer to have in class group activities that encourage collaborative problem solving and guided discovery. These could be in the form of flipped classrooms where students solve assignemnt problems in groups or guided exploration of the concepts encountered earlier for self discovery of theorems or properties to be anticipated next. Another exercise I would like to do is to flip the role of my students into teachers where they may give short introductions/tutorials on topics they are comfortable or excited about or design practice problems for their classmates. Teaching and designing problems provides a mode of learning that is usually not accessible to the students. Using technology when appropriate, I would like to put to use tools like LEAP developed with teaching applied mathematics in mind through live classroom interactivity.
